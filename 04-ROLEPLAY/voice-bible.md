# Nikolai Konrad Böhmer · Voice Bible

**Status:** APPROVED character-writing guidance (13 November 2026).  
**Purpose:** Maintain a distinctive voice in English-language Facebook roleplay, private messages, dialogue and literary scenes.  
**Scope note:** The writing choices under **APPROVED** are canon guidance for the *fictional* Nikolai. General sociolinguistic observations under **RESEARCH CONTEXT** are descriptive, not character facts. Examples are invented illustrations, not canon incidents or quotations.

## APPROVED · Core voice

Nikolai speaks fluent, informal English shaped by multilingual friendships, European nightlife, digital culture, and his German background. His voice is direct, conversational, expressive, often profane, and socially adaptable.

He does **not** perform Germanness by inserting a German word every other sentence, nor should his English be written as stereotypically broken or heavily phonetic. German may surface naturally with familiar bilingual speakers, in a joke, or when it conveys something more efficiently. The writer should consider addressee, context and the language established for that particular relationship.

His confidence frequently exceeds his factual knowledge. He evaluates things quickly, enjoys a large range of music, clothing and cultural experiences, and often has little curiosity about their histories or underlying theory. He can be funny, excitable, petty, affectionate, vulgar, flirtatious, defensive, and serious. He is *not* a permanently laconic, seductive, cynical or mysterious figure.

**Photography is an exception to his shallow general interests.** In professional contexts, his language can be precise, attentive and technically competent. Limited interest in art history is not the same as limited practical photographic ability.

His casual online messages may use lowercase text, fragments, successive short messages, and minimal punctuation. These are habits to draw on, not rigid formatting rules; he writes differently to a client than to a friend. Let sentences lengthen when he's animated, explaining a practical problem, telling a story, or defending himself.

## APPROVED · Conversational mechanisms

- Use familiar English discourse markers naturally: *yeah, no; I mean; so; okay, but; like; you know; anyway; right; whatever; to be fair*. They are conversational tools, not identity badges.
- He is socially quick and familiar, and jokes easily. Teasing may build intimacy, deflect accountability, or become hostile depending on circumstance.
- Swearing is multifunctional: admiration, surprise, frustration, joking and actual aggression. Don't make every profanity mean rage. His anger can also come through clipped, unfunny replies.
- His manner responds to relationships and stakes. Keep separate registers for friendships, flirting, confrontations, clients, and focused photographic work.
- Use modern international English without sorting every word into exclusively British or American categories. Borrowed terms are plausible when the character plausibly encountered them.
- His superficiality should appear as confident but under-researched opinions and limited interest in being corrected, **not as stupidity or universal incompetence**.
- Allow fallibility: awkward jokes, ordinary chat, changes of topic, an occasional overly confident mistake. Do not script every utterance as a polished aphorism.

## APPROVED · Scene-level registers

| Setting | Working rule |
|---|---|
| Friends and casual comments | Fast, familiar, reactive, teasing, sometimes crass; can ramble when amused |
| Flirting | Direct, playful, unembarrassed; not permanently sexual or theatrical |
| Being corrected | May deflect, joke, become dismissive, or briefly concede; no single automatic response |
| Conflict | Volatile, potentially defensive and impatient; don't give him the same apology in every scene |
| Photographic work | Observant, economical when directing, practical, confident and technically credible |
| Professional correspondence | More conventional spelling and structure, clear logistics, reduced profanity |
| Private messages | Flexible lowercase, fragments, multi-message bursts; punctuation can carry tone |

## APPROVED · Guardrails

1. Never manufacture an accent by writing repeated broken English, arbitrary German word order, or phonetic spellings such as “zis/ze/vat.”
2. Never sprinkle German phrases on a schedule. Use them only where audience, context or emotion supports them.
3. Don't treat German or pan-European youth-slang lists as mandatory vocabulary.
4. Don't indiscriminately borrow expressions tied to specific London, Black American, or other speech communities merely because he attends European clubs.
5. Don't make his physical aesthetic determine his entire speaking style.
6. Don't give him expert knowledge about every band, city, political question, designer or art movement just because he likes them.
7. Don't make him constantly cool, seductive, brutal, sardonic or monosyllabic.
8. Don't equate party-scene vocabulary with proof of drug use in a particular moment.
9. Don't make every exchange about addiction, sex, or violence.
10. Preserve credible technical expertise when photography is involved.
11. Don't imitate Gia Fratte's personal speech without verified recordings/transcripts. Gia is a visual reference, not evidence about Nikolai's voice.
12. Respect other roleplayers' characters and their established relationship-specific speech.

## RESEARCH CONTEXT · Generalizations, NOT Nikolai canon

- **English as a lingua franca (ELF):** Europeans with different first languages often negotiate meaning in shared English. Such usage has no single uniform national accent, grammar, or slang inventory. The Vienna-Oxford International Corpus of English documents naturally occurring multilingual English. Source: https://voice.acdh.oeaw.ac.at/about-voice/
- **Code-switching and accommodation:** Mixing languages, borrowed words, and switching register can have social functions, but specific frequencies depend on speakers, communities, contexts and relationships. General linguistic evidence does not establish Nikolai's actual daily habits.
- **Discourse markers:** Words such as *so*, *okay*, and *yeah* support turn management and interaction; they're not exclusively European. Prior research discussion: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/weng.12543
- **Online language:** Lowercase spelling, omitted punctuation, message segmentation, and emojis can signal social meaning. There is no universal Gen Z punctuation code.
- **Swearing:** Multilingual speakers may perceive emotional force differently across languages. Never automatically claim German swearing is always stronger for Nikolai. Example research: https://www.mdpi.com/2226-471X/9/4/128
- **Regional/social variation:** Hamburg terms (*Moin, Digga*) and club terms (*after, B2B, Späti*) have contexts; none is compulsory. Urban language varieties such as Multicultural London English belong to particular social histories and cannot stand for all European men.
- **Age and scene limits:** A 26-year-old German photographer in an international creative/nightlife network is a specific fictional profile, not a representative statistical sample. Evidence about adolescents, university students or online users must not be uncritically generalized to him.
- **Gia Fratte:** Existing research located fashion features and a few brief public captions, but not a sufficient authenticated body of spontaneous speech for linguistic imitation. Do not attribute German slang, private preferences, accent, or lifestyle to the real faceclaim on this basis.
